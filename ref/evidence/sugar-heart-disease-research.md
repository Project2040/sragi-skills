# Sugar: an industry-funded review of heart disease research

**Status:** documented · **Level:** 1 and 3 · **Sources checked:** 14 October 2026

## In short

In the 1960s, a sugar industry trade association funded a scientific review of diet and heart disease, set its objective and saw drafts, without that role being disclosed. The review played down evidence against sugar and pointed to dietary fat.

## What the sources show

Researchers analysed internal documents from the Sugar Research Foundation and published their findings in 2016 ([Kearns, Schmidt and Glantz, *JAMA Internal Medicine* 2016;176(11):1680–1685](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5099084)). According to the article:

- The Sugar Research Foundation funded a literature review on diet and coronary heart disease, published in the *New England Journal of Medicine* in 1967. It paid the authors a total of USD 6,500, about USD 48,900 in 2016 dollars.
- The foundation set the review's objective, contributed articles and received drafts. Its funding and role were not disclosed in the published review.
- The review concluded that reducing dietary cholesterol and saturated fat was the only dietary intervention needed to prevent heart disease. It discounted evidence linking sucrose to heart disease.
- The authors conclude that the foundation's first project on heart disease was designed "to downplay early warning signals that sucrose consumption was a risk factor", and recommend that policy committees give less weight to food industry–funded studies.

## What this shows

An industry with money at stake could shape the scientific picture without the public knowing, and the effect lies in what was left out of the review. It is a clear example of why it matters who pays for research, and why that must be disclosed.

## What this does not show

The authors name the limits themselves:

- There is no direct evidence that the sugar industry wrote or changed the manuscript. The evidence that it shaped the conclusions is circumstantial.
- The documents come from one trade association's archive.
- It does not show that dietary fat is harmless, or that sugar alone causes heart disease.
- It does not show that nutrition science today is captured by industry.

## For your own health

Do not change your own treatment based on this. Talk to a doctor or someone you trust.
